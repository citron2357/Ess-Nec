In his 1994 paper \textit{Essence and Modality} Kit \citeauthor{Fine1994-FINEAM-2} argued against the view that essence should be understood in terms of \textit{de re} modality. He suggested instead to understand metaphysical necessity in terms of essence of things. The idea to explain, especially to ground modality in essence was followed by philosophers such as Hale and Lowe, albeit with some differences in details. I term all such accounts \textit{essentialism} on modality.
\par
To ground modality on essence, essentialists have to provide an account on essence that does not rely on modal notions. And at the same time, they must also derive the necessity of $p$ from the essentiality of $p$. Some philosophers argue that this has never been successfully established. The arguments provided for the latter implicitly assume some modal characteristic on the essentiality of $p$, which is to fail to satisfy the former requirement. And if the essentiality of $p$ has no modal import at all, there seems to be a gap between what it is to be essential and what it is to be necessary, so that the derivation from essentiality to necessity is impossible to achieve. 
\par
In this paper, I will argue that essentialists can reply to the challenge of gap without compromising their core theses. One remaining possibility is overlooked: the gap is bridged by the essence of \textit{to be necessary}. More specifically, I argue that both requirements can be satisfied by assuming that it is essential of to be necessary that if something is essentially true, then it is necessarily so. 
\par
In section 1, I will summarize the problem of the source of necessity, and argue all essentialist accounts give non-transmissive explanation to necessity, even if it was not explicitly stated.
The challenge of gap will be elaborated in detail in section 2. I will argue in section 3 that any attempts on non-transmissive explanation would ultimately appeal to some account on the essence of to be necessary, including of course essentialism. And then I will also show how the criticisms presented in section 2 can be replied in section 4. In section 5, I will clarify why it is legitimate for essentialists to take my suggestion into account. Especially, I argue that it is not circular, at least viciously. Note that I am not trying to argue for the essentialist answer for the problem of the source of necessity, against other alternatives.

\section{The Source of Necessity and Non-Transmissive Explanations}
%------------------------------
For any necessary fact $p$, we can ask \textit{why} is $p$ necessary. It is different from the question of why $p$ obtains. For example, we can answer the question of why it is the case that $3^2=9$ or snow is white by saying that it is the case that $3^2=9$. But this cannot be the answer for the question of why it is necessarily the case that $3^2=9$ or snow is white. 
\par
Similarly, the question of the source of a \textit{particular} necessity should be distinguished from the question of the \textit{general} source of necessity. The particular necessity of the previous fact might be explained by the necessity of $3^2=9$. This however does not guide us at all about the general source of necessity, since the necessity of $3^2=9$ is left unexplained. 
\par
\citeA{Blackburn1993-BLAMAM-6} argued that the general source of necessity cannot be explained by some true propositions. Suppose we want to claim that $\Box p$ because $q$. $q$ would be either necessary or contingent. If it is necessarily true, then it cannot be the final source of necessity, for it leaves the necessity of $q$ unexplained. But it also cannot be the case that $q$ is contingently true, for ``... then there is strong pressure to feel that the original necessity has not been explained or identified, so much as undermined'' (Blackburn 1993, p.53)
\par
Blackburn wishes to further conclude that therefore any realist conception on necessity must fail. This advance might be too hasty \cite{Cameron2010-CAMOTS-3}. However, it is also argued that to explain necessity via contingently or necessarily true proposition is possible. \citeA{Hale2002-HALTSO-27} pointed out that denying the contingency horn presupposes that necessity is never contingent, which some philosophers, notably conventionalists would disagree. But Hale himself took the other option. He advocates what he calls ``non-transmissive'' explanation on modality. %One example of a transmissive explanation of modality is mentioned earlier: $\Box$($3^2=9$ or snow is white) \textit{because} $\Box3^2=9$ and $\Box p \rightarrow \Box(p \lor q)$. Here not only the truth of $3^2 = 9$ but also the necessity of it is used for the explanation, therefore transmitting the necessity from the explanandum to the explanans.
A transmissive explanation takes the form of $\Box p$ \textit{because} $\Box q$, where $q$ itself cannot explain $\Box p$. Perhaps Blackburn is correct if this is the only available form of explanation via necessary truths. Such explanations may help us to sort out some ``fundamental'' necessities from non-fundamental ones, but does not give us the general source of necessity. 
\par
Non-transmissive explanations, on the other hand, take the form of $\Box p$ \textit{because} $q$, where $q$ is necessarily true ``but in which what explains the necessity of the explanandum is not $q$'s \textit{necessity}, but its \textit{truth} simpliciter.'' (Hale 2002, p.311) Here the regress does not occur. Since $q$ is also a necessary truth, the necessity of $\Box q$ would also be explained via the truth of a proposition which is necessarily true, say $r$. But that does not imply that the necessity of $p$ is thus explained by $r$. For, it is the truth simpliciter of $q$ that explains the necessity of $p$, not its necessity. 
\par
Therefore, it suffices as an account for the general source of necessity if we succeed in giving a non-transmissive explanation for all necessities, or at least all fundamental ones. This is usually done by specifying the particular kind of truths which explain the related necessities. Hale's preferred view is that “Necessarily $p$ because $\Box_{X_1,...,X_n}p$” (\citeauthor{Hale2013-HALNBA} 2013, p.152): the necessity of any proposition $p$ is explained by it being essential to some objects $X_1,...X_n$.\footnote{I use two terms ``true in virtue of the nature of'' and ``essential to'' interchangeably, following Fine and Hale.} There are other alternatives. For instance, possible world realists would assert that %the necessity of any proposition $p$ is explained by $p$ being true at all possible worlds. Now, if the notion of possible worlds can be specified without modal notions, this is a genuine case of a non-transmissive explanation:
given that $p$ is a necessary truth, it is necessary that $p$ is true at all possible worlds. But what explains $\Box p$ would be the truth of $p$ being true at all possible worlds, not its necessity. I will classify all those theories of the source of necessity which utilizes non-transmissive explanation as ``non-transmissivism''.
\par
Before proceeding, let me clarify myself between various senses and kinds of essence. In most cases, by ``essence'' I mean \textit{mediate and consequential} essence, following Fine (1995a). Suppose we have a clear idea of a strict and direct essence, which is immediate and constitutive. Mediate essence of $F$ then additionally includes the nature of things that are involved in the nature of $F$; consequential essence of $F$ includes what logically follows from the nature of $F$. I always include mediate essence throughout the paper, but I will occasionally restrict myself to mediate and constitutive essence. I will make myself clear when the shift occurs. 
%\footnote{That is, when I say `constitutive essence' or `consequential essence', it means mediate and constitutive essence or mediate and consequential essence respectively}%
\par
Also crucial is the distinction between objectual essence and predicational essence. On the one side there is the essence of a particular object, and the other side the essence of to be such and such. Fine (2015) argued the essence of to be $F$ is different from the essence of the concept $F$. Suppose the objectual essence of the concept \textit{concept} includes the fact that it is a concept. Then it is a consequential essence of the concept \textit{concept} that $\exists x (x$ is a concept). However, it seems quite implausible to think that it is by nature of \textit{what is it to be a concept} that there is something satisfies it. That is, while $\exists x (x$ is a concept) is plausibly an essence of the concept $conept$, it would not be an essence of to be a concept. This, we may say, illustrates the difference between the objectual essence of concept and predicational essence of to be a concept. 
\par
It would be clear that the essence of objects or things would be the objectual essence of that object. And following Fine (1995a, 2015), by $E_{F}A$\footnote{
    I use ``$E$'' as the essentialist operator, substituting Fine (1995a)'s notation $\Box$. ``$\Box$'' is exclusively used as a necessity operator.
} I mean $A$ is the predicational essence of $F$. Objectual essence would be notated by using the \textit{rigid predicate} $\lambda x. x = a$, where ``$a$'' is a name for an object. To say $p$ is an objectual essence of $a$ I write ``$E_{\lambda x. x = a}p$''. I will occasionally write $E_a$ to denote the objectual essence of $a$, but this is merely an abbreviation of $E_{\lambda x. x=a}$. 
\par
Note that there can be slightly different views among essentialists on what is the relation between essence and necessity. For Kit Fine, being metaphysical necessary is \textit{defined} to being true by the essence of \textit{all objects} (Fine 1994, 1995a). The notion of metaphysical necessity is therefore reduced in terms of essence. This implies that Fine would agree that the necessity of $A$ is grounded on $A$'s being the essence of all objects. Following the notation presented in \citeA{Fine2015-FINUFF}, such a definition which gives the constitutive necessary and sufficient condition for to be necessary yields $E_{\bigvee}A \bm{\leftrightarrow}_A \Box A$, where $\bigvee$ is defined as $\lambda x. \top$. That means that something being true in virtue of the nature of all objects is both (partial) ground and essence of something to be necessary. 
\par
Hale, on the contrary, explicitly claims his account to be non-reductive. Although he agrees with Fine that metaphysical necessity is grounded in the nature of things, he also takes modality to be ``fundamental and irreducible'' (Hale 2013, Ch.3). \citeA{Lowe2008-LOWTNO} also suggests that all metaphysical necessity is grounded on essence, where essence just is the nature of an object, not a further entity. But it is not very clear whether Lowe would agree that necessity can be defined in terms of essence. 
\par
However, any sensible essentialist account on modality is non-transmissivist. Since essentialists hold that the necessity of $A$ is explainable by it being objectually essential to something,\footnote{
    \citeA{Fine1994-FINEAM-2}: ``The metaphysically necessary truths can then be identified with the propositions which are true in virtue of the nature of all \textit{objects} whatever'' (p.9, italics added); \citeA{Hale2013-HALNBA}: ``Metaphysical  necessities might plausibly be identified as those which hold in virtue of the nature of \textit{things} in general.'' (p.150, italics added).
} they are automatically committed to the position that they source of necessity is also necessary. Under the standard axioms of the logic of essence, it is a theorem that $E_F A \rightarrow E_F E_F A$, where the predicate $F$ is rigid (\citeauthor{Fine1995-FINTLO} 1995a, p.255, see THEOREM 6 (ii)). That is, if $A$ is essential to some object, the fact that $A$ is essential to the object is essential to the object in question itself. Since essentialists should accept that $p$ is necessary if $p$ is essential to some objects (this will be argued shortly), $E_F A$ which is the source of $\Box A$ must also be necessary; and the source of $\Box E_F A$ would be $E_F E_F A$, which is also necessary. And so on. Also remind that essentialists suggest $E_F A$, not the necessity of $E_F A$ to be the explanans of $\Box A$. Thus, essentialists turn out to be holding that any source of necessity is necessarily true yet what does the explanatory work is its \textit{truth} simpliciter, not necessity explaining the necessity of the explanandum. In other words, all essentialists are also non-transmissivists.

\section{The Gap between Essence and to be Necessary}
%--------------------------------------
One constraint on all essentialist accounts is that the notion of essence should not be understood in terms of modal notions. For example, suppose for $Fa$ to be the essence of $a$ is just to be the case that $a$ necessarily have $F$. The source of necessity of $Fa$ then cannot be that $Fa$ being essentially true, for to hold that just amounts to say $Fa$ is necessary because $a$ necessary has $F$, and that leaves the \textit{de re} necessity unexplained. It is parallel to the reason why possible world realists should not define possible worlds as worlds that are metaphysically possible. But if essence should be understood independently of necessity, how can one guarantee that all essential facts are necessary?
\par
All essentialists must accept that all objectually essential truths are necessary. To acknowledge contingent objectual essence, one must give a criterion demarcating them from necessary objectual essences without appealing to the notion of essence. That is, one must identify the non-essentialist source of the necessity of necessary essences, which contingent essences lack. And this apparently undermines essentialism. 
\par
Some philosophers question how can essentialists guarantee all essential truths are necessary. To put it formally, essentialists are committed to what \citeA{Mackie2020-MACMBE} calls ``Necessity Principle (NP)'': 
\begin{itemize}[label=(NP)]
    \item $\forall F \forall A (E_F A \rightarrow \Box A)$\footnote{This formalization follows \citeA{Fine2020-Reply}.}
\end{itemize}
where $F$ is any rigid predicate and $A$ is any sentence. I will occasionally omit one or two universal quantifiers for convenience and readability. 
\par
One common understanding of essence among essentialists is by \textit{real definition}: roughly, the essence of certain thing or to be such and such is captured by their real definition. (Fine 1994, p.2; Hale 2013, p.132; \citeauthor{Lowe2012-LOWWIT-2} 2012, p.919). Alternatively, \citeA{Correia2017-CORGEA} suggested an understanding of essence in terms of generalized identity, expressed by the identity statements such as ``for a thing to be a bachelor is for it to be an unmarried adult male'' or ``for the Atlantic Ocean to be filled with water is for it to be filled with $H_2O$ molecules'' (p.643). Here being $F$ expresses the full essence of being $G$ if and only if to be $G$ is to be $F$; it expresses partial essence of being $G$ if and only if there is some $H$ such that to be $G$ is to be both $F$ and $H$ (pp.649-50)\footnote{
    Here again the difference between objectual and predicational essence will be captured by the difference between rigid predicates and non-rigid predicates. Suppose the $Con$ is the predicate \textit{to be a concept}. And using $\equiv$ as the generalized identity operator, $Con(x) \equiv_x p$ states that $p$ is the full predicational essence of to be a concept; $[\lambda x. x=Con]y \equiv_y q$ states that $q$ is the full objectual essence of the concept \textit{concept}.
}. The charge is that there seems no direct evidence that the nature of real definition or generalized identity can ground (NP) alone. How can essentialists ``deliver a modal rabbit out of a non-modal hat'' (Mackie 2020, p.252)?
\par
Hale tried to give an argument. Suppose $\Phi \alpha$ is true in virtue of the identity of $\alpha$. Now suppose it is possible that $\lnot \Phi \alpha$. It implies that there is a $\beta$ such that $\alpha = \beta \land \Diamond \lnot \Phi \beta$. But $\beta$ cannot be identical to $\alpha$ if it is the case that $\lnot \Phi \beta$; for it does not satisfy the condition to be identical to $\alpha$, which is $\Phi$. Therefore $\lnot \alpha = \beta$, contradiction. Since contradictions are impossible, we can conclude that if $\Phi \alpha$ is true in virtue of the identity of $\alpha$, $\Phi \alpha$ is necessary (Hale 2013, p.133). 
\par
But it has been pointed out that the argument does not successfully address the skepticism toward (NP) (\citeauthor{Romero2019-ROMMIN} 2019, pp.125-7; \citeauthor{Leech2021-LEEFET-2} 2021, pp.893-5). For the proposed argument to succeed, it must be assumed that $\Phi$ is \textit{necessarily} the condition for some arbitrary objects to be identical to $\alpha$. Either $\Box(E_{\alpha} \Phi)$ or $\Box \Phi \alpha$ is required. But what is assumed is only that $\Phi$ is \textit{actually} the condition of the identity. To hold that the assumption implies $\Box \Phi \alpha$ simply begs the question. And while $E_{\bigvee}(E_{\lambda x. x = \alpha} \Phi)$ is provable from $E_{\lambda x. x = \alpha} \Phi$, the inference by Finean definition from $E_{\bigvee} A$ to $\Box A$ again begs the question = for that is just another form of (NP). Moreover, Hale himself would not agree to the definition.
\par
In fact, one can construct a proof for (NP) once she accepts the Finean definition of necessity. It is an axiom in the logic of essence that $F \subseteq G \rightarrow (E_F A \rightarrow E_G A)$, where $R \subseteq G$ is for $\Pi x (Rx \rightarrow Gx)$ (Fine 1995a, pp.246-7), and $\Pi$ is a quantifier ranging everything in the domains. Since for any rigid predicate $F$ $F \subseteq \bigvee$, it follows that $E_F A \rightarrow E_{\bigvee} A$. And since $\Box p$ is just defined as $E_{\bigvee} p$, we can derive $E_F A \rightarrow \Box A$, which is (NP) itself. But there is another reason to regard the definition to be unsatisfactory (Romero 2019, p.129-36). As it was mentioned, Fine defines necessary truths as propositions that are true by the essence of all objects. However, by ``all objects'' he means all objects in the domain, which is the set of all possible objects. Persuasively, since the problem of which objects are possible depends on the notion of modality, to conceive necessary truths the essence of all \textit{possible} objects is circular. In the same paper Fine also writes ``[...] one might operate with a broader conception of possible object than is customary in modal logic'' (ibid, p.244), but it is still unclear what the ``broader conception of possible object'' is. 
\par 
To understand essence in terms of generalized identity may seem to offer better prospects for advocating (NP), since ``[a]s with objectual identity, generalized identity is tightly linked to metaphysical necessity'' (\citeauthor{Correia2017-CORGEA} 2017, p.656). Following Correia and Skiles, let ``$p \equiv q$'' denote the generalized identity between $p$ and $q$. Here $p \equiv p$ is a theorem\footnote{For they take generalized identity to be reflexive (p.645)} and therefore in modal logic it follows that $\Box (p \equiv p)$. And given $p \equiv (q \land r)$, which means that $q$ is a partial essence of $p$, by substitutability we get $\Box (p \equiv (q \land r))$. That is, assuming $q$ is a partial essence of $p$, it follows that \textit{necessarily} $q$ is a partial essence of $p$. And a full essence of $p$ is trivially also a partial essence of it.
\par
This actually proves something stronger than (NP): namely, $E_F A \rightarrow \Box E_F A$. However, since anything true in virtue of the nature of some object would be true \textit{simpliciter}, this stronger consequence implies (NP). The problem here is that the proof presupposes $\Box (p \equiv p)$: that the generalized identities are necessary. What is the source of the necessity of $(p \equiv p)$? It has been argued that the necessity of $\Box (p \equiv p)$ cannot be explained by the essence of generalized identity (Leech 2021, pp.901-2; cf. Romero 2019, p.138). For example, suppose that the necessity is explained by that \textit{for two things to be generically identical is partially to bear a reflexive relation}. Grant, but what explains the necessity of the partial generalized identity between generalized identity and reflexive relation? Here we cannot appeal to the necessity of $p \equiv p$, for that exactly is the supposed explanandum. Therefore, to appeal to the essence of generalized identity itself cannot serve as the final source of the necessity of all generalized identity. 
\par
Leech seems to conclude that in explaining the necessity of the generalized identity ``\textit{one should not appeal to the essence of anything to account for this necessity}''. She further considers this constraint as ``the crucial and general objection'' on the necessity of essence (p. 902). 
\par 
I disagree. I think Leech overlooks one remaining possibility: that generalized identities are necessary because of the essence of \textit{to be necessary}. Furthermore, in my view, this also applies directly to (NP). Every truth essential to something or all things are also necessarily true, not because of the nature of essence but because of the nature of what is it to be necessary. In section 3, I will argue that essentialists must accept that (NP) is a partial essence of to be necessary - the thesis I will call (NPEN). It will also be shown in section 4 that challenges presented in this section can be suitably replied by (NPEN). In section 5 I will argue that this thesis is legitimate.

\section{Non-transmissivism and the Essence of to be Necessary} %----------------------------------
I argued in section 1 that all essentialists are non-transmissivists. Non-transmissive explanations, as mentioned, take the form of $\Box p$ because $q$, where $q$ is also necessarily true. Usually, the role of $q$ will be taken by a proposition \textit{about} $p$. Some examples would be that $p$ \textit{is true in all possible worlds}, or that $p$ \textit{is true in virtue of the nature of something}, and so on. Let $C$ be any such status or criterion a proposition can satisfy. A non-transmissivist theory on the general source of necessity will then give an account of what the status $C$ is. Thus, non-transmissivists would generally hold that $\Box p$ \textit{because} $C(p)$, where $p$ necessarily satisfies $C$. Essentialists are those who hold that the status $C$ is \textit{to be objectually essential}. 
\par
All non-transmissivists should affirm $\forall p (C(p) \rightarrow \Box p)$ for the same reason why essentialists must accept (NP). If there are some true propositions that satisfy $C$ but are not necessarily true, it means that there are something additional to $C$ that makes propositions satisfying $C$ necessary. $C$ is then at most only a part of the criteria a proposition must satisfy to be necessary, contrary to the initial assumption. 
\par
Therefore, the problem of gap is not peculiar to essentialists; it turns out to be a general difficulty for any non-transmissivist account. For example, a possible world realist would say that $\Box p$ because $p$ is true in all possible worlds. But if the notion of possible worlds is defined without modal notions, one can equally ask, in Mackie's term, how possible world realists can ``deliver a modal rabbit out of a non-modal hat''. The general difficulty is to provide an elucidation of how a non-modal understanding of $C$ can get along with the requirement of $\forall p (C(p) \rightarrow \Box p)$. I will call this  ``General Necessity Principle (GNP)''.
\par
How can a non-transmissivist guarantee (GNP)? First, one \textit{cannot} merely appeal to some fact about $C$ or necessity that is not essential of to satisfy $C$ or to be necessary, but take $C(p)$ to be the ultimate source of $\Box p$. Suppose a proposition satisfying $C$ is necessary as long as $C$ satisfies other non-essential criteria, say $X$. That is, (GNP) depends on $X(C)$, but $X(C)$ is not a partial essence of to satisfy $C$ or to be necessary. Then, what \textit{explains} $\Box p$ is not merely $C(p)$ but $C(p) \land X(C)$, since $C(p)$ cannot guarantee $\Box p$ alone. Additional component $X(C)$ is required because the nature $C$ does not by itself ensures that. So the account that $\Box p$ because $C(p)$ fails, for to satisfy $C$ turns out not to be everything that is needed to explain necessity. Similarly, we cannot appeal to non-essential property of to be necessary. Suppose (GNP) obtains as long as $Y(\Box)$ obtains\footnote{
    Here I take the operator $\Box$ to be a constant higher-order predicate which is equivalent to ``to be necessary'' in natural language.} 
and that is neither an essence of to satisfy $C$ nor to be necessary. The correct account then should be that $\Box p$ because $C(p) \land Y(\Box)$, and the original account turns out to be untenable. To sum up, if one wants to insist that $C(p)$ provides full explanation for $\Box p$, one cannot think (GNP) obtains regardless of the nature of to satisfy $C$ or to be $\Box$.  
\par
The previous argument does not show it cannot be the case that $X(C)$ or $Y(\Box)$ grounds (GNP); it shows that even if one believes that $X(C)$ or $Y(\Box)$ grounds (GNP), in order to be a non-transmissivist, one should also take the essence of to satisfy $C$ or to be necessary is sufficient to guarantee (GNP). However, non-transmissivists also cannot take (GNP) to be a constitutive essence of to satisfy $C$. Suppose for the contrary that $E_C (C(p) \rightarrow \Box p)$. Now, if one takes the notion of essence as closely tied to the notion of real definition, it follows that to satisfy $C$ is partially \textit{defined} in a way such that it is the case that $C(p) \rightarrow \Box p$. Or following a revised version of definitional essentialism (Fine 2015), it follows that $\Box p$ is constitutive necessary condition for some $p$ to satisfy $C$. It seems to me, then, to satisfy $C$ depends partially on to be necessary, and therefore $C(p)$ cannot ground $\Box p$. 
\par
Perhaps the argument can be made clearer by introducing Fine's notion of ontological dependence. According to his account on ontological dependence (\citeauthor{Fine1995-FINXD} 1995b), $x$ depends on $y$ “if $y$ is a constituent of a proposition that is true in virtue of the identity of $x$” (p. 275). Therefore from $E_C (C(p) \rightarrow \Box p)$, it follows that to satisfy $C$ ontologically depends on $\Box$, i.e., to be necessary. Of course, the possibility of $\Box$ depending upon $C$ is still open. But what non-transmissivists need is not a mere dependency, but that to satisfy $C$ is strictly prior to to be necessary, which prevents to satisfy $C$ being defined, or constituted in terms of to be necessary.
\par
The only remaining possibility, then, is to say that (GNP) is the essence of to be necessary. Any non-transmissivist account on necessity must take their relevant instances of (GNP) to be true because of the essence of $\Box$. And in fact, although not explicitly stated, Fine's account implies that. As I mentioned earlier, Fine \textit{defines} $\Box$ in terms of essence: $E_{\bigvee}A \bm{\leftrightarrow}_A \Box A$. And its implication $E_{\bigvee}A \bm{\leftarrow}_A \Box A$ is just another, intuitive way to put $E_{\Box} \forall A(E_{\bigvee}A \rightarrow \Box A)$ (Fine 2015, p.299). I also mentioned that for any rigid predicate $F$ $E_F A \rightarrow \Box_{\bigvee} A$. Since from $E_{\bigvee}A \rightarrow \Box A$ and $E_F A \rightarrow \Box_{\bigvee}A$ it follows that $E_F A \rightarrow \Box A$; we can get $E_{\Box} \forall F \forall A(E_F A \rightarrow \Box A)$. 
\par
But I also mentioned that Finean definition is accused to be circular, for he presupposes the domain of \textit{all metaphysically possible} objects. An additional concern is that we might want to talk about the essence of \textit{impossible} objects, which are not in any broad sense of ``possible object'' one of them. Typical examples are the Russellian set or the biggest prime number. Why is the Russellian set impossible? An essentialist line of argument might be something like the following. The Russellian set is essentially a set of all sets that does not contain itself; therefore it is necessary that for every $x$, if $x$ is identical to the Russellian set, it is a set of all sets that does not contain itself. And it is also necessary that, for any $x$ such that it is the set of all sets that does not contain itself does not exist. Therefore, Russellian set necessarily does not exist, i.e., is an impossible object. But for such an argument to be sound, it should be held that the essence of an impossible object can also be the source of some necessities. 
\par
If essentialists include the essence of impossible objects as the source of necessity, how can the Finean definition be maintained? One way out would be to construct a domain which includes both possible and impossible objects. But which objects, possible or impossible, are there? Perhaps Meinongians could give us an answer.\footnote{
    See, for example \citeA{Zalta1983-ZALAOA} or \citeA{Priest2005-PRITNT}. Fine himself also worked on the problem of non-existent and the comprehension principle on objects. The negative part, which he criticizes certain kinds of object theories, was published in \citeA{Fine1982-FINTPO-3}. There he also stated that the positive project where he develops his own theory will be published in part II of the paper, but unfortunately it remains as a non-existent object till now.
}
However, I think it is an unnecessary detour to think that essentialists would need a theory of objects.\footnote{
    It is even unclear if an independently developed object theory would get along with essentialism well. For example, Zalta, although I see no logical inconsistency between his theory of objects and essentialism, advocates a modal conception of essence specified in terms of his theory of objects in \citeA{Zalta2006-ZALEAM}.
} 
It is not very desirable for essentialists to just wait until object theorists come up with a comprehension principle of objects that every essentialist can happily endorse. 
\par
Instead, I suggest discarding the original Finean definition, and take (NPEN)\footnote{\textbf{N}ecessity \textbf{P}rinciple as the \textbf{E}ssence of to be \textbf{N}ecessary}: 
\begin{itemize}[label=(NPEN)]
    \item $E_\Box \forall F \forall A(E_F A \rightarrow \Box A)$
\end{itemize}
not as a consequence of the definition, but directly into the essentialist account on necessity. Here again $F$ range over only rigid predicates. If we follow definitional conception on essence, (NPEN) amounts to say that (NP) is part of the definition of \textit{to be necessary}, not the definition of \textit{to be essentially true}. The Finean intuition that to be necessary is to be the essence of \textit{all things} would then be preserved under any suitable theory of objects, as long as the axiom $F \subseteq G \rightarrow (\Box_F A \rightarrow \Box_G A)$ is employed. Of course this generalizes. For any non-transmissivists, the generalized version - (GNPEN): $E_\Box \forall p(C(p) \rightarrow \Box p)$ - must be taken into account. 
\par
Since essentialists are one specific kind of non-transmissivists, not only Fine but all essentialists must accept (NPEN). Otherwise, either essence turns out not to provide a full explanation for necessity; or we face the disturbing consequence that to be essential ontologically depends on to be necessary. Therefore, any compelling argument for essentialism would also be the argument for (NPEN). And this is not peculiar to essentialists. For example, possible world realists must argue that \textit{if something is true in all possible worlds then it is necessary} is the partial essence of to be necessary. For I do not aim in this paper to argue for the essentialist answer for the problem of the source of necessity against other alternatives, I will not explore possible specific arguments for (NPEN).

\section{How to Bridge the Gap}
%----------------------------
What I \textit{should} show in this paper, is how essentialists can answer the challenge raised by Romero, Leech and Mackie by utilizing (NPEN). To briefly reiterate, their main criticism was that once we stick to the notion of essence that is \textit{not} defined in terms of necessity, there is a gap between essence and necessity, for there seems no good reason to think all essential truths are necessary. That is, we cannot ensure (NP) by non-modal conception of essence, which seems to be crucial if essentialists want to explain all necessity by essence. My suggestion is, of course: the gap is to be bridged in by the nature of to be necessary. 
\par
Let us return to Hale's argument for the necessity of definitional essence. I take Romero's criticism to be partially correct, to the extent that we cannot infer $\Box \Phi \alpha$ from $E_\alpha$ without taking the essence of to be necessary into the argument. We cannot appeal only to the essence of $\alpha$ to establish $\Box \Phi \alpha$. So there is a gap between the essence of \textit{objects} and \textit{de re} necessity. The gap, however, can be bridged by the nature of to be necessary. So the revised argument goes as follows. Suppose again, that $E_\alpha \Phi \alpha$. Now, Romero and Leech ask: why $\beta$ such that $\Diamond \lnot \Phi \beta$ cannot be identical to $\alpha$, if the initial presupposition was only that it is actually $E_\alpha \Phi \alpha$? Now we have a further reply for that. Since by the definition of $\Diamond$, we can instead assume $\lnot \Box \Phi \beta$.
%\footnote{ Perhaps one would prefer to have $\Diamond$ as primitive and $\Box p$ to be defined as $\lnot \Diamond \lnot p$. It is certainly possible for essentialists to follow that, the source of \textit{possibility} of any possible truths $q$ is explained by there being no rigid predicate $F$ such that $\lnot q$ is the partial essence of $F$. Any other arguments surrounding essentialism can be reconstructed in such way.}
Suppose for contradiction that $\alpha = \beta$ and therefore by substitutability $\lnot \Box \Phi \alpha$. 
\par
Now, from $E_\Box \forall F \forall A (E_F A\rightarrow \Box A)$ we can prove, utilizing the \textit{T-axiom}: $E_F A \rightarrow A$ (Fine 1995a, pp.247-8) and modus ponens that $\forall F \forall A (E_F A\rightarrow \Box A)$. By universal instantiation we get $E_\alpha \Phi \alpha \rightarrow \Box \Phi \alpha$; and by the assumption $E_\alpha \Phi \alpha$ and modus ponens we get $\Box \Phi \alpha$. Contradiction. Therefore, there can be no $\beta$ such that $\alpha = \beta \land \Diamond \Phi \beta$, which ensures that if $\Phi$ is actually an essential property of $\alpha$, then it is a metaphysically necessary condition for something to be identical to $\alpha$. 
\par
It is important to notice that the argument depends on the essence of to be necessary, and the logical rules of the essentialist operator, the universal quantifier and the material conditional. So we do not suppose objectual essences are \textit{ab initio} necessary. The necessity is derived from the essence of to be necessary and the relevant logical rules\footnote{
    Which is common to expect to be (objectually) essential to the relevant logical operators. For example, Fine (1994) writes ``the logical necessities can be taken to be the propositions which are true in virtue of the nature of all logical concepts''. At least every instance of the logical rules would be essential to the logical concepts, and even the rule itself if it can be taken as a higher-order proposition.
}. This fact will be used later in section 5.
\par
This is nevertheless not to ignore the essentialist intuition that it is in some sense ``obvious'' that objectual essences are necessary. I suggested that essentialists cannot take objectual essence to be \textit{ab initio} necessary. However, it seems also true that there certainly is some kind of frustration when essentialists are confronted with questions like “why are facts of objectual essential necessary?” or “why an object necessarily has the property that defines what the object is?” This is, I suggest, same kind of frustration when one is confronted with questions like ``why is water $H_2 O$?'' or ``why does Socrates' singleton set have Socrates as member?''. According to \citeA{Glazier2017-GLAEE-2}, these questions are subject to essentialist explanation.\footnote{Glazier restricts himself into immediate and constitutive essence. However, his account on essentialist explanation would be able to naturally expand to the mediate essence except the point that essentialist explanation is ultimate.} We can simply answer: ``that is just what water is'' or ``that is just what the set is''. Essentialists therefore can take (NPEN) as an explanans of such an essentialist explanation of (NP). ``Why are objectual essences necessary?'' I suggest one kind of answer essentialists can give is: ``that is just what necessity is!'' 
\par
So much for the definitional conception of essence. We can accept a proposition is necessarily true because it is true in virtue of the nature of \textit{some} - instead of \textit{all} - objects. To be objectually essential guarantees to be necessarily true partially because it is the essence of \textit{to be necessary} that if a proposition is essential to some objects, then it is necessarily true. To vindicate the necessity of a particular essential fact, one must appeal to the essence of to be necessary and other logical rules.
\par
What if essence is understood in terms of generalized identity? We can translate the same idea into $\exists q (\Box p \equiv_p (\forall x \exists r ([\lambda y. y=x]z \equiv_z (p \land r))) \land q))$. In short: to be necessary \textit{just is} to be a partial essence of some object. We can now proceed similarly as we did in the case of definitional essence. How can we argue here that therefore all objectually essential facts are necessary? Suppose we have a statement of objectual essence: $[\lambda y. y = a]x \equiv_x (s \land w)$. $s$ is a partial essence of the object $a$. And it follows from the nature of to be necessary that for $s$ to be necessary is just to be a partial essence of $a$. Since $s$ is a partial essence of $a$, we get that $s$ is necessary. 
\par
Interestingly, here we have another option. Recall that in section 2, the discussion on the essentialism in terms of generalized identity was questioning not (NP) but $\forall p \Box (p \equiv p)$. Can we account for this stronger thesis than (NP) instead? I think yes. Since we cannot say $\Box (p \equiv p)$ is true by the essence of the generalized identity, the core idea again is that it has something to do with the nature of to be necessary. 
\par
But we still should be careful on the formulation. For example, $\exists q(Nx \equiv_x \forall p(\Box (p \equiv p) \land q)$ might seem natural; but it would not work. For one thing, does not yield $\exists p \Box (p \equiv p)$, for the supposition is consistent with there being no necessary truths. It is also quite mysterious why would a particular fact having necessity involves that any fact of generalized identity is necessary. 
\par
Instead, I suggest the following: $\exists q (([\lambda G. Gx \equiv_x \Box x]F) \equiv_F (\forall p \Box(p \equiv p) \land q))$. This, however, is not to say it is the predicational essence of to be necessary that $\Box (p \equiv p)$; but rather that it is the partial objectual essence of necessity operator that $\Box(p \equiv p)$. In this case, it is not involved in the essence of some particular proposition to be necessary that any fact of generalized identity is necessary; and $\Box(p \equiv p)$ is guaranteed, for $\Box p \equiv_p \Box p$ is a theorem. Now essentialists can proceed their argument as it was suggested in section 2. We can by substitutability get $\Box(p \equiv (q \land r))$ and $\Box(p \equiv q)$, which means that the fact on what is essential to what is necessary.

\section{Circularity}
%-----------------------------------------
I argued in the previous section that the only viable option for non-transmissivists is to take the relevant instances of (GNP) or something stronger than that to be a partial essence of to be necessary. This was done by showing that any other alternatives are incompatible with non-transmissivism. But to say that is only viable option for non-transmissivists is not to say it is tenable. After all, non-transmissivism might also be incompatible with (GNPEN); and with the argument I presented it completes a trilemma which likely undermines any kind of non-transmissivism. 
\par
It seems that there are no \textit{logical} inconsistency with the thesis and non-transmissive explanation on necessity. To say the nature of to be necessary is such and such is one thing, and to say the necessity of necessary truths is explained by such and such factor is another. Similar to essentialism, which is an instance of non-transmissivism. 
\par
But it could be the case that the thesis seriously undermines the theoretical purpose of non-transmissivism. The reason to reject (GNP) being essential to $C$ was not logical inconsistency. The argument against it was that it results in the disturbing consequence in respect of fundamentality. 
\par
(NPEN) would not face the problem of fundamentality. It implies that to be objectually essential is more fundamental than to be necessary. But perhaps there are other problems. 
\par
Romero (2019) states six constraints on the notion of explanation which the `explanation' of modality by essence must satisfy: 
\begin{itemize}[align=left]
    \item[\textbf{Why}] The explanation must answer to a `why' question.
    \item[\textbf{Acyclicity}] The explanation should not be circular.
    \item[\textbf{Fundamentality}] The explanans should be metaphysically more fundamental than the explanandum.
    \item[\textbf{Ontic and Factive}] It must be the case that the truth of the explanans, not our concept or theory of it, explains the truth of the explanandum.
    \item[\textbf{Structural}] The explanandum need not be an object or a property.
    \item[\textbf{Objectivity}] The explanation should be objectively valid. (p.124)
\end{itemize}
It is already shown that (NPEN) does not make essentialism infringe \textbf{Fundamentality}. Essentialism with (NPEN) also easily satisfies \textbf{Why}, \textbf{Ontic and Factive}. It answers the question ``\textit{Why} is $p$ necessary?", by appealing to the \textit{facts} about essence of things, not our \textit{concept} of essence. Essentialists would certainly hold that if the essentialist explanation on necessity is valid, then it is \textit{objectively} valid. Finally, \textbf{Structural} is not a negative constraint that one can infringe. 
\par
However, whether essentialism with (NPEN) is compatible with the constraint on \textbf{Acyclicity} is perhaps not so obvious. Some might feel uncomfortable to the idea that the nature of to be necessary validates the explanation from essence to necessity. But if it is indeed circular, what exact part of the picture contains a circle?
%\par
%As a reminder, the outline of the picture I am proposing is as follows. First there is the essentialist thesis: for any necessary truth\footnote{Alternatively, any fundamental necessary truth.} $p$, $\Box p$ because $p$ is essential to some objects. It would be obvious that then the notion of essence should not be understood in terms of necessity. But then there is a gap. Essentialists want to hold that, on the one hand the necessity of \textit{every} necessary truth can be such explained, and on the other hand we do not need additional explanatory components to account for the necessity. But then essentialists commit themselves into (NP). But how can essentialists guarantee (NP), if they understand the notion of essence \textit{without} the notion of necessity? And the answer for that is my final thesis: (NP) is true in virtue of the nature of \textit{to be necessary}.% 
\par
Notice that I do not try to explain the necessity of $p$ by (NPEN). The explanatory work is done by the fact that $p$ is essential to some objects. The urge to account for the implicit assumption of (NP) arises only when essentialists are asked how \textit{all} necessities are explained by essence. (NPEN) rather explains why we can generalize the point. In particular cases of the explanation of the source of necessity, the nature of to be necessary remains irrelevant.
\par
Hence, there is at least no blatant circularity. Actually, it is even unclear why involving the essence of to be necessary in particular explanations would be viciously circular. Suppose I am wrong in the previous paragraph, and every explanation on necessity must include (NPEN). $p$ is necessary because $p$ is essential to some objects \textit{and every such truths are necessary in virtue of the nature of to be necessary}. What is wrong with this kind of explanation? My point can perhaps be made by a simple analogy. If someone asks me why 3 is a prime number, I can simply answer ``because 3 is a natural number that is not a product of two natural numbers smaller than itself.'' However, if someone thinks this is incomplete, then I can add: ``... \textit{and} every such natural numbers are prime in virtue of the nature of to be prime''. It would be obvious that neither explanation for the fact that 3 is prime is viciously circular. 
\par
More intriguing problems regarding circularity target specifically  (NP): how is (NP) necessary? But it needs to be clarified whether essentialists are really committed to the necessity of (NP). I have formulated the account in a way that avoids such a commitment. Essentialists must hold that every fact essential to some \textit{objects} is necessary. However, my proposal was that (NP) is the \textit{predicational} essence of to be necessary, not an objectual essence of, for example the concept of necessity. Thus, (NPEN) does not force essentialists to admit that (NP) is necessarily true. Notice that by doing so one does \textit{not} abandon the necessity of necessity: $\Box p \rightarrow \Box \Box p$. This is again because $E_F A \rightarrow E_F E_F A$ is a theorem where $F$ is rigid.
\par
However, there certainly are some circumstances where essentialists are automatically committed to the necessity of (NP). One obvious case is that if one insists the (NP) is true in virtue of the nature of the logical object of necessity. In this case (NP) itself commands (NP) to be necessary. Similar point can be made if one understands essence in terms of generalized identity, and prefers to take $\Box(p \equiv p)$ to be the partial objectual essence of  necessity. This is the option considered in the end of the fourth section. If an essentialist prefers that option, by the necessity of generalized identity one would be committed to $\Box \exists q (([\lambda G. Gx \equiv_x Nx]F) \equiv_F (\forall p \Box(p \equiv p) \land q))$.
\par
Or, some essentialists might wish to include the essence of to be necessary in all explanation on source of necessity. Here the explanans of the necessity of $p$ would be the conjunction of the essentiality of $p$ and (NPEN). Since essentialists try to give a non-transmissive explanation, the conjunction should also be necessary, although the explanation does not appeal to the necessity. And since for a conjunction to be necessarily true each conjunct should be necessarily true; (NPEN) should be necessary. And it would be obvious that if (NPEN) is necessary, also is (NP).\footnote{
    Actually, this is quite a disturbing consequence because it is \textit{not} a theorem in the logic of essence that $\Box_F A \rightarrow \Box_F \Box_F A$, where $F$ is a non-rigid predicate such as ``to be necessary''. I think the suggestion that (NP) should be involved in source of all necessity goes better rather with the alternative that (NP) is true in virtue of some \textit{objects} related to necessity. However, that already gives us a reason to take (NP) to be necessary. 
} 
\par
Now let us temporarily grant that (NP) is necessary, and therefore by essentialism (NP) is essential to some objects. Let such an object be $n$. Now, it does not pose a serious problem if (NP) is the essence of to be necessary independently of being the essence of $n$. But perhaps it is also plausible to suppose that (NP) being the essence of $n$ grounds (NP) being the essence of to be necessary. Therefore, (NP) holds ultimately because of the nature of $n$, and my suggestion collapses to the thesis that the gap between essence and necessity is bridge by the nature of $n$. 
\par
The puzzle is then as follows. Essentialists will account for the source of $\Box$(NP) as $E_n$(NP). Now it may seem there is a gap between $E_n$(NP) $\Box$(NP), which must be bridged by $E_n$(NP). That is: $E_n$(NP) grants $\Box$(NP) because $E_n$(NP). This is apparently circular, or amounts to say there is no gap at all rather than to bridge the gap. And I admitted that there is a gap. 
\par
One answer to the alleged puzzle is, of course, to deny (NP) is necessarily true. But even we grant the necessity of (NP), I think there is a possible reply. Recall the argument from $E_{\alpha} \Phi \alpha$ to $\Box \Phi \alpha$ in section 4. The essentiality of (NP) is indeed one crucial premises of the argument, but not the sole premise. The proof utilized $T$-axiom of the logic of essence and the inference rule modus ponens and universal instantiation. And it is quite customary to suppose the instances of logical axioms and inference rule is essential to the relevant logical notions (Fine 1994, 1995a). Therefore, one possible answer to the puzzle is to say, in the most rigorous sense, the answer to the problem of why $E_n$(NP) grants $\Box$(NP) should appeal to not only the essence of $n$ but also the essentialist operator, conditional and universal quantifier. 
\par
One might feel uncomfortable to speak of the essence of essentialist operator $E$. For one thing, did not I reject to regard (NP) to be true in virtue of the essence of essence considering fundamentality? Yes, but no ``modal axioms'', that is, axioms governing the essentialist operator involve notions on necessity or possibility. So to say modal axioms are essential to essence does not result to the anti-essentialist consequence. It nevertheless does suggest that essence is ontologically dependent on logical connectives, set-theoretical notions, or some notions represented by the metalanguage of logic. This, however, is what essentialists \textit{can} admit. Fine, for instance, clarifies himself as holding essence to be ``methodologically primitive'', which is ``perfectly compatible with its not actually being a primitive.'' (Fine 2020, p.465)

\section{Conclusion}
%-----------------
I argued that essentialists must accept (NPEN) - that (NP) is true in virtue of the nature of to be necessary. This follows from the more general character of non-transmissivism. Not only essentialist theories on the source of necessity, but all non-transmissivist theories face the problem of the gap. When one asserts that $p$ is necessary because $C(p)$, one has to elucidate the notion of $C$ without modal notions, and at the same time grant (GNP): $\forall p (C(p) \rightarrow \Box p)$. I argued in section 3 that all non-transmissivists should regard (GNP) to be a partial essence of to be necessary. And since all essentialists theories are non-transmissivist - this is argued in section 1 - essentialists are forced to accept (NPEN). I also tried to show that this account is not circular as it may first appear.
\par
It is worth noting that (NPEN) seems to yield the conclusion that essentialists cannot hold the notion of to be necessary to be metaphysically fundamental. It should be ontologically dependent to the notion of to be essentially true of something. Therefore, if Hale meant metaphysical fundamentality by ``fundamental and irreducible'' character of necessity, his position seems no longer tenable. As long as he does not argue for the implausible view that necessity is independent of the predicational notion of to be necessary, either he must abandon essentialist theory on the source of necessity or abandon the fundamentality of necessity. 
\par
What I did \textit{not} argue in this paper is that essentialism is superior to its competitors. My aim in this paper has been to show that the particular challenge to essentialists can be met coherently without dismissing the challenge. Although what the challenge from gap points out is correct, it is not strong enough to defeat essentialism. Therefore, the conclusion of the paper is merely conditional: \textit{if} essentialism is correct, then so is (NPEN). I mentioned that any argument in favor of essentialism is also an argument for (NPEN). But of course the contrapositive holds. Any criticism against (NPEN) would thereby be a criticism of essentialism. Dealing with those criticisms, however, would exceed the scope of this paper.
